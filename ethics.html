Ethics

My research builds attacks in order to improve defences. That is a position that needs stating rather than assuming, so here it is, along with what this site does with your data.

Research

Why build the attacker

A defence that has never been attacked is an assumption. Adversarial evaluation is how a detection claim becomes a measurement, and it is standard practice in the security venues I work toward. When I describe building attackers, I mean building them in a lab, against systems I am authorised to test, to find out what a defender would actually have missed.

Scope and authorisation

Offensive work happens only against systems I own, systems belonging to my lab, or systems where the owner has authorised the testing in writing. The penetration testing in my professional history was contracted work with a defined scope. No technique on this site is demonstrated against anyone else's infrastructure.

Disclosure

If my work finds a vulnerability in software other people run, I report it to the vendor or maintainer first and give them reasonable time to fix it before saying anything publicly. Where a coordinated disclosure policy exists, I follow it. If we disagree about the timeline, I would rather be late than be the reason something gets exploited.

What I publish

I publish methods, measurements, and detections. I do not publish working exploits for unpatched software, and I do not publish tooling whose main use is harming people who did not consent. The line is not always obvious, and I would rather discuss a specific case than pretend a general rule settles it.

What is on this site

The project descriptions are illustrative and say so. They describe approaches, not client systems, and contain no customer data, no real findings, and no detail from work covered by an agreement.

What this site does with your data

Nothing. There is no analytics, no tag manager, no tracking pixel, no cookie, no localStorage, no sessionStorage, and no server-side log I can read, because the site is static files on GitHub Pages and I do not operate the server.

There is one exception, and it only happens if you ask for it. The radio on the front page plays through YouTube, and pressing play hands your visit to Google. Nothing about it runs until you press play. The section below sets out exactly what changes when you do.

The one page that looks like an exception

There is a page on this site that reads your browser and tells you what it found. It is a demonstration of how much a page learns without asking. Every value is computed in your browser, displayed to you, and then forgotten when you close the tab. None of it is sent anywhere, including to me. Open the network panel in developer tools while that page loads and you will see it stay empty.

Decoder progress

The decoder tracks which hidden messages you have solved. That list is held in memory for as long as the tab is open and is never written to your device. Reload the page and it is empty again. It would have been easier to store it, and storing it would have made the claim above untrue.

No third parties, until you press play

Every file this site loads comes from this site. The typefaces are served from here rather than from Google Fonts, which is the usual quiet exception on a page that claims to collect nothing. Archivo and IBM Plex are both under the SIL Open Font License, so hosting them is simply a matter of doing it. There is no CDN, no comment widget, and no tag manager.

The radio

The radio is an embedded YouTube player, and an embed is the thing this page would otherwise tell you to be wary of. So it is built to load nothing until you choose it. On a normal visit the page holds a list of track names and no player at all: no iframe, no script from Google, no request leaving this site. Open the network panel and read the front page top to bottom and it stays as empty as every other page here.

Pressing play changes that, and it is worth being exact about how. The player script loads, the iframe is created, and your browser contacts youtube-nocookie.com, youtube.com, google.com, googlevideo.com, gstatic.com, ytimg.com, ggpht.com and googleapis.com. Google therefore learns your IP address, your user agent, and which video you played, and if you are signed in it knows who you are. I use the privacy-enhanced domain, which keeps the advertising hosts out of that list and holds cookies back until playback starts. It reduces the exposure. It does not remove it, and I would rather say so than let the word nocookie do work it cannot do.

The alternative was to host the music here, which would have meant serving other people's recordings without the right to, on a public repository, until somebody filed a notice and the whole site came down. Embedding is the arrangement the rights holders actually agreed to. Putting it behind a deliberate press is the part I can control.

How the bars follow the music

They do not listen to it. A page cannot read the audio out of a cross-origin frame, and should not be able to: sound is content, and a page that could read it could read the pictures too. So each track is analysed here, once, before it is ever published. What ships is a contour, a few dozen kilobytes of numbers describing how loud each part of the frequency range is over time. The bars replay that contour in step with the player's own clock. No audio from your session is captured, analysed, or sent anywhere, and nothing asks your browser for a microphone or for your screen.

What I cannot promise

GitHub serves these files, so GitHub sees the requests, including your IP address. Their logging is their business and outside my control, and moving the hosting would only change whose logs you are in. That is the one dependency left, and I would rather name it than let "this site collects nothing" quietly stand in for "almost nothing".
